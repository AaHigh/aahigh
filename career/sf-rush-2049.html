San Francisco Rush / Rush 2049

1997–2003 · Atari Games / Midway · Lead Programmer

Lead programmer on the Rush 2049 arcade (and Special Edition). Also involved across the earlier Rush lineage (The Rock, Wavenet) and the console ports (Dreamcast, N64).

WORLD WIDE BEST PLAYER — the SHA-256 system

After the Wavenet cash-prize work, the Rush 2049 verification was built for something larger than cash: www.rush2049.com WORLD WIDE BEST PLAYER. The economic benefit to the company was the ability to highlight the top player. It was also a conscious nod to Ed Logg and the three-letter-initials high-score culture that defined the arcade era.

In late July / early August 1999 the production schedule was down to the wire. SHA-256 was required and no clean, ready-to-use implementation was available on the public internet. Boss John Ray authorized secretary Karen to type the algorithm from the blue book Applied Cryptography. Her transcription contained errors. While debugging those errors, a clean source was located on a USENET newsgroup via groups.google.com (the practical “crutch” of the era — pre-Wikipedia, pre-broadband, still the wild west). The correct SHA-256 code was locked into the coin-op codebase in time for the required testing window so the game could still launch on schedule.

Personal roots

The high-score obsession started early. At age ten, attending Holiday Heights Elementary and playing at the Stop-N-Go in North Richland Hills, Aaron could post a 99,990 on Asteroids any day of the week, or finish a session with ten or more free ships after a couple hundred thousand points with very few deaths.

Decades later he commissioned Clay Cowgill to create a “single-ship ROM” for Asteroids: extra-ship awards disabled and initial lives reduced by two. Combined with a high-score save kit (compatible with the mod), the setup produces a pure measure of skill. The mechanics of the game stay identical; only the penalty for a mistake becomes extreme.
